GREENSBORO, N.C. — The piano in Earl McGrath’s garage has eighty-seven working keys. The missing note is an E-flat. He knows exactly which songs require it, exactly where his hand has to move instead, and exactly why his grandmother refuses to let him replace the instrument.

She played it for forty years. He learned on it after football practice, still wearing the wristband with his plays printed inside. Somewhere between a stubborn left pedal and a stubborn right tackle, McGrath developed a habit: when the first answer disappears, find another one without stopping the music.

Utah would like that habit in its quarterback room.

The Utes lead the pursuit of the **four-star quarterback**, ranked **No. 81 nationally, No. 3 in North Carolina and No. 9 at his position**. Tennessee is second, with Clemson, USC and TCU also in the first five. McGrath remains uncommitted. At 6 feet and 193 pounds, he offers the kind of movement and improvisation that can make a defense regret winning the first three seconds of a play.

## The wrong kind of film room

McGrath’s father restores old radios and amplifiers out of the garage. His mother works nights as a respiratory therapist. Earl learned to solder before he learned to shave, and his first serious purchase was a used cassette deck with one functioning speaker.

He records his piano sessions on it. The mistakes stay on the tape.

That explains the stranger half of his football library. Most recruits keep their touchdowns. McGrath keeps a folder called “Bad Ideas”: sacks he could have avoided, open receivers he missed, scrambles that ended with him trying to outrun a sideline. On Sundays, he watches them without sound, writes down the decision he should have made, then watches again to see when the mistake actually began.

His favorite play in that folder is a throw he never made. He escaped two rushers, crossed the field and discovered a receiver alone behind the defense. By then, he had crossed the line of scrimmage. The scramble gained four yards. The lesson occupied three notebook pages.

## A flood, a borrowed helmet and a new position

Before quarterback, there was safety. Before safety, there was a summer storm that flooded the family’s garage and left McGrath hauling ruined equipment to the curb with his father.

He salvaged a small keyboard, dried it for a week and got it working. He still plays it before road games.

That fall, his school’s junior-varsity quarterback broke a collarbone. McGrath volunteered, borrowed a helmet with a different facemask and spent his first practice throwing too hard to receivers who were standing too close. The position stuck. So did the safety’s instinct to study where everybody was going before deciding where he belonged.

Now he wants to study electrical engineering. He also wants to build an amplifier small enough to fit in a locker and loud enough to annoy an entire offensive line.

## Why Utah is listening

Steve Smith Jr.’s first Utah offense has already shown how much a quarterback can contribute with both his arm and his feet. Devon Dampier’s opening win supplied an appealing picture of what that job can look like. McGrath would be a prospect to develop within that approach, with his own decisions still to sharpen.

Utah has the early advantage, not a signature. Tennessee and Clemson give him serious alternatives, and a lead in September leaves plenty of recruiting season ahead.

Back in Greensboro, the old piano is staying. McGrath says he can work around one missing note.

The question is which college will get to hear the next song.
