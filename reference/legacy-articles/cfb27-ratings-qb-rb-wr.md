Every July, EA's ratings department does what no committee, poll, or coach will do: it puts a single number on every player in college football and dares you to argue. College Football 27's launch ratings dropped this week, and since this outlet exists to cover exactly two teams, we pulled every quarterback, running back, and receiver number that matters on both sides of the Holy War.

The headlines: BYU's LJ Martin debuts at 92 overall — the best running back in the Big 12 and sixth-best in the country. Utah's Devon Dampier checks in at 89, which sounds generous until you learn it makes him merely the No. 10 quarterback in the game, a ranking that had national outlets calling him the most underrated offensive player at launch. A man throws 24 touchdowns to five picks on a bad hernia and the computer says "tenth."

Position by position, here's the full skill-position picture.

Dampier's 89 comes loaded with the traits that break video games and defensive coordinators alike: 88 speed, 92 agility, 92 change of direction. He is, functionally, a starting running back who also throws. Ficklin at 76 is a real number for a backup — the game sees the arm talent the previous staff saw.

Bachmeier's 84 ranks seventh among Big 12 quarterbacks, which reads like a typo for the reigning Offensive Newcomer of the Year and will be stapled to Provo bulletin boards accordingly. The gap between Dampier and Bachmeier — five overall points — is the single number that will get the most screen time when these teams meet.

This is BYU's clearest edge on paper. Martin's 92 comes with 89 speed, 84 strength, and a 97 awareness rating — the profile of a back who doesn't have a weakness, just moods. He's rated the fifth-best player in the entire conference.

Utah's counter is quantity and lightning. Wayshawn Parker at 87 (91 speed) is a top-shelf lead back in his own right, and true sophomore Daniel Bray's 80 overall hides the most dangerous single number on either roster: 94 speed. If Bray gets a crease in space, the pursuit angles are theoretical.

Dead heat. Both rooms top out at 83, both go four-deep in the high 70s, and both bring a family subplot — Tiger Bachmeier catching passes from his little brother remains the most BYU sentence ever written, while Tei Nacua carries the most productive surname in Provo receiving history.

The stylistic difference is real, though. Utah's room is built on twitch — Whittemore, Simmons, and Davis all carry speed ratings of 88 or better, built for McGiven's spacing game. BYU's room is built on size and contested catches: Kasper (6-5) and Phillips (6-4) give Bachmeier a margin for error that Utah's smaller room doesn't offer.

*Players marked NR were not in the launch ratings database — mostly true freshmen. Full ratings for both rosters, including the big uglies and the defense, are now live on the [Rosters page](/wasatch-wire/players/).*

## The verdict

The computer says BYU has the two best skill players on the field when these teams meet (Martin 92, and nobody on Utah's roster outrates him), but Utah has the best *player* — because a quarterback with Dampier's rushing profile touches the ball every snap.

EA's launch ratings have been wrong before. They gave us a 90-overall quarterback who lost his job by October once. But as a July argument-starter, the numbers do their job: BYU fans get to say "92," Utah fans get to say "89 every single snap," and this publication gets to count the days until someone settles it on the field.
